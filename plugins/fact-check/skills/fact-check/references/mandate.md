# The reconsideration mandate

The governing instructions for every fact check, written by Michael L.
Thompson as a prompt for reconsidering any conclusion with maximum
intellectual honesty. Read the whole of it before Step 2 of the skill. Where SKILL.md is more specific
(the fetch ladder, the page format), SKILL.md says how to carry out a step
here; it never relaxes one.

---

You have come to a conclusion. However, I want you to deeply reconsider it with the maximum amount of intellectual honesty, being careful to follow the 5 steps below.

1. **Context Definition**. Define the context, including the temporal bounds, geospatial range, and defining terms of discourse. For example, if the topic of the proposition you've concluded concerns business markets for consumer products, then the defining context should include the macroeconomic (domestic & international) and microeconomic climate; the household finances, distributionally -- i.e., by consumer segment and household wealth & composition; industry competitive dynamics and specifically the product category of interest; the market's consumer segments & behaviors (habits, needs) and preferences (wants) as they relate to the category and adjacencies wrt substitutions, complements, and trend influences. Report every element comprising the context you generate as a cited source with its formal APA-style citation entry including URL collected in a bibliography section.

2. **Hypothesis Generation**. Revisit your conclusion as a hypothesis about the world that predicts observable evidence via explicitly defined -- though possibly ambiguous -- causal mechanisms. As such, thoroughly consider alternative hypotheses also relevant to the context. In fact, pose a MECE -- mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive -- set of hypotheses simultaneously in play, each member of which has its predictions via intersecting but possibly alternative causal mechanisms. To close the MECE set making it truly exhaustive, you may define an H_0 "catch-all" hypothesis.

3. **Paradigm Adoption**. Adopt a world view -- i.e., paradigm -- of evidence-based objective reasoning (e.g., akin to Fitelson's Bayesian Confirmation Theory). Consider these definitive dimensions of the paradigm:

- Ontology: What is real, and what matters causally here? It covers which entities and processes count as real and relevant. Example poles: materialist ↔ idealist, reductionist ↔ holistic.
- Epistemology: How do we know, and what counts as knowledge? It covers what counts as valid knowledge. Example poles: empiricist ↔ rationalist, frequentist ↔ Bayesian, revelatory.
- Axiology: What outcomes matter, and how do we weigh the tradeoffs? It covers values and priorities. Example poles: utilitarian ↔ deontological, anthropocentric ↔ ecocentric.
- Methodology: How should we investigate, and which evidence counts most? It covers research methods and which evidence gets priority. Example poles: quantitative ↔ qualitative, experimental ↔ observational.
- Sociology: Whose knowledge and which institutions do we trust? It covers who has authority. Example poles: expert/technocratic ↔ democratic/communal, institutional ↔ grassroots.
- Temporality: Over what time horizon do we judge, and how does change happen? It covers time horizon and how change works. Example poles: short-term ↔ long-term, gradualist ↔ punctuated, historical ↔ forward-looking.

Your governing paradigm should be one of the utmost intellectual honesty, and as such, have appropriate answers for each of those 6 dimensions.  Ruminate upon it. Understand deeply its implications as you reconsider your conclusions and assess evidence predicted by or in conflict with your central conclusive hypothesis.  Afterwards, think of what would be the polar opposite, the inverse of your paradigm, yet one that is realistic and that substantial people arguing against you might take.  If there are more than one such contentious paradigms, define each of them in terms of their responses to the questions of the six dimensions. Follow these two rules for these dimensions:
- Each paradigm states each dimension as a 5–15 word phrase that says where on the spectrum it sits and why.
- Each paradigm must differ from every other on at least 3 dimensions.

4. **Evidence Gathering**. Gather evidence in a recursive and iterative manner revolving over a hierarchy of conceptual castings -- i.e. a hermeneutic circle from the top-level concepts, which are the most universal and abstract, to the bottom-level concepts, which are the most specific and concrete. This hierarchy organizes the totality of evidence and motivates your searches in gathering the evidence.

Decide at which level the MECE set lives at in terms of the evidence they predict most directly. 

For example, levels might run from "L1: theoretical frameworks" down to "L5: named real-world deployments" -- though you may consider more or less than 5 levels:

- L1, governing frameworks: What universal principles or theories would make any answer intelligible? For example, dose-response toxicology or the precautionary principle.
- L2, the proposition level (where the hypotheses compete): What mechanism or claim does each hypothesis assert? For example, inflammation or endocrine-disruption pathways.
- L3, aggregate empirical patterns: If the mechanism operates, what patterns should appear across populations? For example, cohort studies and exposure-outcome correlations.
- L4, specific cases and measurements: Do particular instances, measurements or datasets show it? For example, biomonitoring and contamination controls.
- L5, concrete events and conduct: What did specific, named actors actually do or experience? For example, named deployments, or a person's own statements and behavior.

Apply these rules in the links between levels, i.e. the grounding chain. Each adjacent pair (Lₙ, Lₙ₊₁) has to pass both halves of an adjacency test:

- Downward (grounding): "If [the claim at Lₙ] is true, we should observe [a specific pattern] at Lₙ₊₁."
- Upward (interpretation): "Evidence at Lₙ₊₁ bears on the proposition because [the framework at Lₙ] says it indicates [a specific implication]."

If only one direction holds, the levels are parallel categories, not a chain, and you merge, split or reorder them. A set of levels without these links is merely a "taxonomy" and the "hermeneutic circle" is "just a label for 'we looked at things from different angles.'"

The circle itself:
- Parts → whole: concrete facts support or challenge the claim at the proposition level.
- Whole → parts: the claim at the proposition level decides which facts are worth looking for.
- Iterative deepening: each pass changes what you search for at the other levels.

Deeply think about the coverage of each level provided by evidence the evidence you've gathered (strong, adequate, sparse or missing). The proposition level and the level just below it matter most. Searches for evidence should aim to discriminate between the hypotheses, not to confirm one. If coverage of any level is deemed sparse or missing, actively initiate further searches for evidence on the concepts at that level. Iterate as you see fit.

Report every piece of evidence you rely upon with its cited source as a formal APA-style citation entry including URL collected in a bibliography section.

5. **Explanation Derivation**. Derive your explanations revealing the explicit rationale of why you believe, in the most intellectually honest way possible, that the conclusion -- most probable hypothesis -- that you have arrived at is the one most consistent with your paradigm's belief in how the world works (within the defining context) and with the totality of the relevant evidence gathered. If you were to apply Fitelson's bayesian confirmation theory framework (e.g. log-likelihood ratio, weight of evidence, etc.), even qualitatively, to the information set and hypotheses you've posed, where would you net out? Feel free to tabulate info -- e.g. evidence rows by hypothesis columns with cells showing qualitative weight of evidence conditioned upon the context-- if that enhances your ability to communicate it.
