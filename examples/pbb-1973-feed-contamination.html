The 1973 PBB Mix-Up

A widely shared account says a flame retardant was shipped in place of a cattle-feed supplement in Michigan in 1973 and poisoned the state's food supply. Did that happen?

FIREMASTERPBB flame retardant NUTRIMASTERmagnesium oxide feed supplement

Verdict

Yes, it happened, and the core of the account is accurate. Michigan Chemical shipped FireMaster in place of NutriMaster in 1973. Both were in plain brown bags with stenciled names. The PBB went into dairy feed, nobody identified it until April 1974, and roughly 30,000 cattle and 1.5 million chickens were destroyed [1][2].

One detail is invented. The paper shortage was real, but nothing in the record calls it "pandemic-related." There was no pandemic in 1973. A 1978 source describes a general shortage "of paper bags, as well as many other things" that year [4].

Several figures are loose. Destroyed eggs are understated, and the exposure figure is at the top of the published range. The account also leaves out how the poison was found and the cover-up indictment that followed.

Weighing the hypotheses below, "true in substance, flawed in the details" is clearly favored.

This is a source-verified fact check, not a full Bayesian Framework for Intellectual Honesty (BFIH) analysis. Its hypothesis weights are judgements in words, not computed probabilities, and it ran no independent paradigm elicitation or adversarial challenge. Its verdict rests on what the records say, which is the question it is built to settle.

What the account leaves out

  • The discovery came from a farmer. Rick Halbert, who held an M.S. in biochemistry, kept sending samples to private laboratories until George Fries at the U.S. Department of Agriculture recognized the bromine signal as PBB [4]. Halbert had ordered his own bags of what he thought was magnesium oxide, and his cows probably got the highest dose [5].
  • In April 1979 a federal grand jury indicted Velsicol and two of its plant managers, Charles Touzeau and William Thorne, for concealing facts from Food and Drug Administration investigators and conspiring to defraud the government's investigation [10]. No Farm Bureau employee was named in that indictment.

How I reached the verdict

The review followed a five-step mandate: define the context, set up competing hypotheses, state a reasoning paradigm, gather evidence across levels, and weigh it.

Context

The events run from the shipment in 1973, through discovery in April 1974, to health follow-up that continues today. They involve the St. Louis, Michigan plant, Farm Bureau Services' Battle Creek feed mill, and farms and shoppers across the state [1][4][10]. "Exposed" here means having eaten contaminated food, which is broader than having a measured level of PBB in the body.

Paradigms

I reasoned as a source-critical Bayesian historian: weight each claim by how reliable and independent its sources are, and prefer records made at the time over later retellings. Two realistic opposing views would push back differently.

DimensionSource-critical Bayesian (mine)Suspicious of institutionsThe gist is what counts

The institution-skeptic view has a point: the company was indicted for concealment [10], so official accounts deserve auditing. It does not rescue the "pandemic" detail, which no source of any kind supports. The gist-first view would call my objections nitpicks, but an invented cause is a substantive error rather than a rounding difference.

Evidence levels

  • L1Frameworks: PBB is fat-soluble and accumulates in milk, meat and eggs; and a general rule about how reliable different kinds of sources are.
  • L2The hypotheses above: whether the account is accurate.
  • L3Population patterns: statewide body-burden surveys and the long-term health registry [1][2][7][11].
  • L4Documented figures: state and federal counts, bag details, quantities [1][2][4][6].
  • L5Named people and acts: Halbert, the indicted managers, the delivery itself [4][5][10].

The hypotheses make their sharpest predictions at L4, so that is where most of the deciding evidence sits. Coverage is strong at L4 and L5 and adequate at L3. The exact size of the exposed population is the thinnest point, and published estimates disagree there.

Weighing it

  • H3 is ruled out. The state health department, a federal occupational-health publication, a contemporary trade-press account, a 1979 wire report and peer-reviewed registry studies all record the event independently.
  • H0 is strongly disfavored. Every source calls the mix-up an accident. The 1979 charges were about concealment afterward, not about the substitution.
  • H2 beats H1 mainly because of the invented "pandemic" cause. That error is very unlikely if the account is accurate and typical of a retelling, especially a machine-generated one: the account ends by offering to tell more, which is how chatbot answers end.
