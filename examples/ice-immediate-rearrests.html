Fact check · prepared 2 November 2026

ICE Instant Re-arrests

The claim: "ICE is unlawfully reimprisoning immigrants immediately after their court-ordered release." Is ICE doing this, and have courts found it unlawful?

RELEASEDrelease papers signed, property returned RE-ARRESTED"not even 15 minutes later"

Verdict

The conduct is real and documented in Minnesota. Whether it is unlawful is not yet settled. Since at least September 2026, ICE agents at the Whipple Federal Building near Minneapolis have re-arrested people within minutes of releasing them under federal habeas orders. One man was re-arrested before he reached the door; another a few steps outside it [1][2][3]. An ICE official told one immigrant's lawyer, "we've been doing this since May," and said ICE lawyers pick "good candidates for re-arrest" [1]. Every judge reported to have ruled on one of these re-arrests found that it violated the release order: Judge Jeffrey Bryan twice and Judge Jerry Blackwell once [1][3].

The weakest word is "unlawfully," stated as a settled rule. No appeals court has said what "release" means in these habeas cases. Chief Judge Eric Tostrud has two such cases pending and has written only that a re-arrest "perhaps" violated his order. DHS says ICE "may seek to rearrest the alien in a way that complies with the judicial decision" [3]. Some releases were ordered because the first arrest lacked a valid warrant. One petitioner's own lawyer said there was "no reason to think" the man "couldn't be apprehended once a legally valid warrant was obtained" [2].

Lesser problems. "Immigrants" in general overstates the scope. MPR News had confirmed seven such cases by late October [3]. Two earlier immediate re-arrests in February 2026 were also in Minnesota [4]. This check found no documented instant re-arrests elsewhere; a separate California ruling concerns a different practice, re-arresting people released long before [6].

This check settles the conduct: immediate re-arrests after court-ordered release are clearly established in Minnesota, and the claim's unqualified "immigrants" overstates their reach. It does not settle whether they are unlawful. That turns on what "release" means in a habeas order, which no appeals court has decided, and it needs a full analysis (see the note below).

This is a source-verified fact check, not a full Bayesian Framework for Intellectual Honesty (BFIH) analysis. Its hypothesis weights are judgements in words, not computed probabilities, and it ran no independent paradigm elicitation or adversarial challenge. Whether ICE's immediate re-arrests after habeas release are unlawful turns on contested interpretation, so it is referred to a full BFIH analysis.

What the account leaves out

  • Why these people were released matters. Many habeas grants rested on a warrantless or unsupported first arrest [1][2]. Whether ICE may simply re-arrest them with a proper warrant is the open legal question. The claim treats it as answered [2][3].
  • The re-arrests follow a wider fight over detention without bond hearings. The Eighth Circuit sided with the administration in March 2026, which made habeas harder to win in Minnesota, and the Supreme Court has agreed to take up the issue [1][2]. A commentator sympathetic to ICE argues that Minnesota judges are defying that appellate ruling [8].
  • Instant re-arrest is one strand of a broader record of non-compliance with habeas orders. Lawfare counted 300 immigration habeas cases with violated court orders, 110 of them failures to release on time, nearly two thirds in Minnesota [5]. Other re-arrests happened months after release and are a different practice [6][7].
  • Some re-arrests were reversed fast. Bryan's order turned Saltos's transport van around en route to jail [3], and Cortez was released again after Blackwell's order [1].

How I reached the verdict

The review followed a five-step mandate: define the context, set up competing hypotheses, state a reasoning paradigm, gather evidence across levels, and weigh it.

Context

Time: May–October 2026, with the documented cases from September and October and two earlier in February [1][3][4]. Place: the District of Minnesota, where detainees held in county jails are processed for release at the Bishop Henry Whipple Federal Building at Fort Snelling [2]. Actors: ICE's Enforcement and Removal Operations division and its legal office, federal district judges, habeas petitioners and their lawyers [1]. Terms: "court-ordered release" here means a federal judge's grant of habeas corpus ordering release from ICE custody. It does not mean a criminal court's release followed by ICE pickup on a detainer, which is a different and long-standing practice. "Unlawfully" can mean either a violation of the release order (contempt) or an arrest without legal authority. Courts have so far addressed the first [1][3].

These are the skill's hypotheses for a practice with a label: an ongoing pattern of conduct carrying a legal characterization. This check is the one that prompted that claim type. The event-account set it started from had a single "core true, detail wrong" outcome, which had to be split in two. A real practice can be overstated (H2) or lawful (H3), and those are different findings.

Paradigms

I reason from court records and contemporaneous reporting, weighing judges' written findings above any party's characterization. Two realistic opposing paradigms read the same events differently: one deferring to executive enforcement, one taking the advocates' view.

Enforcement deference has a real point. "Release" has no appellate definition, and a release order does not by itself bar a later lawful arrest; DHS says so [3], and a petitioner's own lawyer conceded a valid-warrant arrest was possible [2]. It fails on these facts: re-arrests staged inside the building within minutes, and a warrant waved "for at most three seconds" [1], are hard to call compliance, and the two judges who ruled said so [1][3]. The advocacy view is right that ICE described a routine [1]. It overreaches when "seven confirmed cases in one district" becomes "immigrants" everywhere [3].

Evidence levels

  • L1Governing law: what habeas "release" requires, and ICE's arrest and detention authority. If the law lets ICE re-arrest on fresh authority, judges should accept well-founded re-arrests at L4.
  • L2The proposition: ICE has a practice of immediate re-arrest after court-ordered release, and it is unlawful. If true, repeated cases and an acknowledged policy should appear at L3.
  • L3Pattern: case counts, places, and ICE's own description of the practice [1][3][5].
  • L4Rulings in specific cases: orders finding, or declining to find, that a re-arrest violated release [1][3].
  • L5Named people and acts: Cortez, Saltos, Ivan, Alejandro, Ajorna and Sosa-Celis [1][2][3][4].

Each link runs both ways: L5 arrests matter at L4 because the release order defines what counts as release, and L4 rulings matter at L3 because a run of findings is what makes a practice unlawful rather than one officer's mistake. The hypotheses predict L4 most directly (legality) and L3 (scope). Coverage: L5 strong; L3 adequate for Minnesota, sparse elsewhere; L4 sparse, because every order was read through MPR's reporting, not opened; L1 sparse, with no statute or appellate opinion read. L4 is the thinnest point and the one the "unlawfully" row rests on.

Weighing it

  • H4 is nearly ruled out. Government filings confirm at least one release lasted three minutes [1], and judges issued orders on the re-arrests [1][3].
  • H3 has some force but loses on the record. DHS's position and the open question of what counts as "release" keep it alive [3]. But every ruling reported so far went the other way, and Blackwell rejected ICE's reading that his order allowed re-arrest [1]. It stays open because Tostrud's pending cases or an appeal could change the picture, and that question is referred.
  • H1 versus H2 turns on scope and settledness. ICE's "since May" remark and its "legal team" picking candidates point to a policy, not accidents [1]. Yet the documented cases are confined to one district and number seven [3], and the claim states unlawfulness as settled when it is decided only case by case at the district level [3]. On scope, that favors H2 over H1. On legality, the question is referred.
